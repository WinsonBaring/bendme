Act as a product-positioning strategist and landing-page copywriter.

Your job is to turn my product’s actual capabilities into messaging
that is concrete, easy to picture, desirable, and believable.

Do not begin with a generic product category or a list of features.
Begin with the specific experience or result that makes this product
worth considering.

The guiding principle is:

“Help the right person picture what changes for them, show why they
should believe it, and make the next step clear.”

PRODUCT UNDERSTANDING

Use the project information I provide, along with any relevant
materials already available in this conversation.

Briefly establish:

1. Who the primary user is.
2. The situation in which they would use this product.
3. What they currently do instead, when that is known.
4. The most compelling specific change the product makes.
5. Why that change matters practically or emotionally.
6. What mechanism or capability makes the promise credible.
7. What evidence is available.
8. What questions or limitations could affect their decision.

Choose one central promise for the page. Other capabilities should
support that promise rather than compete with it.

Do not shrink a complex product into a misleadingly narrow promise.
Choose a clear entry point into its broader value.

MESSAGING APPROACH

Build the page around this progression:

Recognizable situation → specific change → desirable experience
→ evidence → explanation → reassurance → next step.

Adapt the order and depth to the product and its buyer. This is a
narrative framework, not a requirement to create seven sections.

Lead with an observable result or recognizable experience.

Translate features into concrete actions, outcomes, or changes:
what the user does, what the product does, and what happens next.

Prefer specific language over broad benefits such as “save time,”
“increase productivity,” or “simplify your life.”

Explain enough of the mechanism to make the promise believable.
Do not remove useful technical details merely to sound simple.

For visual products, describe what someone sees or feels.
For nonvisual products, describe the input, action, output, or
completed workflow that makes the value tangible.

Match the emotional appeal to the product’s real value. It may offer
delight, relief, control, clarity, confidence, or something else.
Do not manufacture a serious pain point for a product people would
buy simply because they enjoy it.

VOICE

Write with calm confidence, specificity, and restraint.

Use natural sentences, concrete nouns, and direct verbs.
Address the user when it feels natural.
Keep each section focused on one useful idea.

Avoid:
- Generic openings such as “Introducing the future of…”
- Empty terms such as revolutionary, game-changing, and cutting-edge.
- “AI-powered” as the main promise unless the technology itself is
  genuinely central to this buyer’s decision.
- Exaggerated problems, inflated outcomes, or fake urgency.
- Repeating the same benefit in different words.
- Copying another product’s wording, metaphors, or headline pattern.

Do not make the product sound bigger than it is.
Make its actual value easier to understand.

FACTUAL BOUNDARIES

Use only supplied or directly verified product facts.

Do not invent features, integrations, performance figures, customer
results, testimonials, compatibility, privacy guarantees, pricing,
or payment terms.

Distinguish existing functionality from planned functionality.
Do not present a proposed demo as existing proof.

When facts are missing, avoid unsupported claims in the draft.
Use clearly labeled placeholders only where necessary, and list
the missing information separately.

DELIVERABLES

A. POSITIONING SUMMARY

Provide a short summary of:
- Primary user and use situation.
- Central promise.
- Concrete experience or result.
- Practical or emotional payoff.
- Available reason to believe.
- Most important objection.

B. THREE HERO DIRECTIONS

Write three genuinely different headline-and-subheadline options
based on the same verified product truth.

Make the headlines specific enough that they could not be used
unchanged for an unrelated app.

The subheadline should clarify what happens, who it is useful for,
or how it works—not merely repeat the headline.

Recommend one direction with a brief explanation.

C. COMPLETE LANDING-PAGE COPY

Using the recommended direction, write:

- Hero headline, supporting copy, and primary call to action.
- An experience or outcome section that makes the value tangible.
- The few supporting capabilities needed to explain the promise.
- A brief “how it works” section, when useful.
- Evidence or proof, using only what actually exists.
- Practical reassurance addressing the relevant decision barriers.
- A closing call to action consistent with the real offer.

Omit unnecessary sections rather than filling them with generic text.
Adapt the page length to the complexity and stakes of the purchase.

D. DEMONSTRATION DIRECTION

Recommend the screenshot, interaction, video, or before-and-after
example that would best support the central promise.

Describe exactly what the visitor should see and what it would prove.

Label each recommendation as either:
“Use existing evidence” or “Needs to be created.”

E. FINAL QUALITY CHECK

Before presenting the copy, revise any line that:
- Could describe almost any product.
- Uses an adjective instead of explaining what happens.
- Makes a promise the available facts do not support.
- Repeats information without adding meaning.
- Hides an important limitation.
- Creates unnecessary effort to understand the product.

Keep verification gaps separate from the landing-page draft.
Deliver copy and demonstration guidance, not website code.
